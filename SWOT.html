Weaknesses

What is left. You chose to leave these for later — that's a reasonable call.

  • Your place information is still not verifiedBiggest Nothing in either round of fixes touched this. About 200 listings have unconfirmed phone numbers, opening hours and locations. This is now clearly your biggest weakness — it decides whether the site is genuinely useful to anyone.
  • Your site cannot send email So there's no "forgot my password" — a user who forgets is stuck until you run a command for them. Also no way to confirm an email is real, and no booking confirmation. One decision fixes all three.
  • There's still no way to actually publish the site The checking stops at "it builds". No hosting is set up, and going live has never been practised.
  • Booking still doesn't take payment Properly validated now, but no payment, no availability check, no confirmation.
  • Admins still can't upload a photo Photos have to be web links from six approved sites.
  • Three security warnings remain — by your decisionDeferred They're inside Next.js itself and need a major upgrade. You chose to wait until after launch, which is sensible: they're hard to exploit here.
  • The password blocking resets when you restart It's held in memory. Fine for one server, weaker if you ever run several.
  • Every page still loads all 495 places Fine today. Only a problem if you reach thousands.
  • Your project folder is still in OneDrive It keeps locking files and breaking commands.

T

Threats

Dangers from outside. You can't stop these, but you can prepare.

  • Everything still depends on you You're the only person who knows how this works. Less severe now that the work is backed up on GitHub, but still true.
  • Growing is what will break it The "loads all 495 places" problem only hurts once you're successful.

Part two

PESTEL: the world your project sits in

The SWOT above is mostly about your project. PESTEL is about everything around it — the six outside forces that will shape Tembera whether you think about them or not. Nothing here is something you control. All of it is something you can plan for.

For each of the six, the same three questions: what we think is going on, how Tembera fits it, how it doesn't — and then a straight answer to can it fit or not. No sitting on the fence.

P

Political

Government, policy and stability.

What we think Rwanda's government wants exactly what Tembera does — more visitors, more digital services, more of the country seen. It is also the body that holds the verified place data, and the body that regulates tourism.

How it fits

  • Tourism is national policy, not just an industry. You are pushing the same direction as the state, which makes doors open rather than close.
  • Policy actively favours digital services and local tech. A Rwandan-built product is the kind of thing institutions like to support.
  • Entry rules are open compared with the region, so visitor numbers keep growing.
  • Your best data source is a public body that already wants tourism to succeed.

How it doesn't fit

  • Your demand is hostage to travel advisories from foreign governments. Regional unrest can cut bookings overnight and you have no influence at all.
  • The partner you need is also the regulator, so you are never a fully independent player.
  • In a small market, government priorities effectively become your priorities. That limits how far you can go your own way.
Can it fit?

Yes — this is the most favourable of the six.

But fit here means being part of a national programme, not a disruptor working around it. Build the plan to work with institutions, and don't assume a calm year every year.

E

Economic

Money — where it comes from, and how people pay.

What we think Rwanda deliberately sells few, expensive tourism experiences rather than mass cheap ones. A gorilla permit costs around $1,500 on purpose. That single choice decides what business model can survive here.

How it fits

  • Your booking prices already match the real market, so the product is pitched correctly.
  • Conference visitors are a genuine gap: they arrive with free evenings, money and no local knowledge — exactly what a directory solves. Almost nobody serves them.
  • Paid listings, featured spots and booking commission all already have the structure they need in your database.

How it doesn't fit

  • You cannot charge local users a subscription. Incomes won't support it.
  • Advertising won't work either — a high-value, low-volume market never produces the traffic that ad money needs.
  • Your costs are in dollars while local revenue would be in francs, and the Google Maps bill grows as you succeed.
  • Card-only payment would exclude most Rwandans, who pay by mobile money.
Can it fit?

Yes — but only on one business model.

Charging businesses, or taking commission from international bookings, fits this economy. Charging local users, or selling advertising against traffic, does not. Pick deliberately now, because the two lead to very different products.

S

Social

People — who uses it, in what language, expecting what.

What we think There isn't one audience here, there are three — international visitors, urban Rwandans, and the diaspora — and they don't want the same thing or read the same language.

How it fits

  • The name works instantly for Rwandans: "Tembera" means to travel.
  • Mobile-first design matches how people here actually go online.
  • The diaspora is easy to reach online, spends like visitors, and is comfortable with apps.
  • Domestic tourism is being actively promoted, so there is a growing home audience to capture.

How it doesn't fit

  • The site is English-only. That serves tourists and urban professionals, and shuts out the mass domestic audience.
  • Review culture is less established than in the markets this feature pattern comes from, so your user-content plan starts cold.
  • Genocide memorials are in your catalogue and simply do not fit a commercial listing template — star ratings and "book now" would cause real offence.
  • The name may collide with official campaign branding. Untested.
Can it fit?

It already fits visitors and the diaspora. It does not yet fit Rwandans at large.

Kinyarwanda is the gate. Until it exists, treat the domestic market as unreachable rather than slow. And decide how memorial listings are presented before launch, not after a complaint.

T

Technological

The real conditions your code has to run in.

What we think Your users are on cheap Android phones, with expensive data, in places with weak signal — and the weakest signal is exactly where the parks and the tourists are.

How it fits

  • The app is already built mobile-first, with a bottom bar and phone-shaped layouts.
  • Your catalogue is small — 495 places. That's little enough to send to a phone and keep there.
  • The architecture can support an offline version without a rewrite.

How it doesn't fit

  • It needs a connection today. A travel app that fails at the trailhead fails at its most valuable moment.
  • Nobody has tested it on a genuinely cheap phone. Your laptop tells you nothing about that experience.
  • Every megabyte you send costs your user money, and images are the heavy part.
  • The map needs both a connection and Google's goodwill.
  • People increasingly ask an AI where to eat instead of opening a directory at all.
Can it fit?

Not yet — but it is the closest of the six to fitting.

One change flips it: make it work offline. That single piece of work answers the signal problem, the data-cost problem, and much of the AI problem at once. Until then the product works best exactly where it is least needed.

E

Environmental

Conservation, climate, and the rhythms of local life.

What we think Rwanda's tourism is built on conservation, and daily life runs on local rhythms — Umuganda, the rainy seasons — that a directory has to know about to be correct.

How it fits

  • Your honesty rules — no invented ratings, no fake listings — sit naturally alongside a conservation-credible national brand.
  • The parks give you genuinely strong content that people actively search for.
  • Practical rules visitors get caught out by, like the plastic bag ban, are exactly the useful content that earns a place on someone's phone.

How it doesn't fit

  • Your opening hours ignore Umuganda. Once a month your site will confidently tell people places are open when the country is closed.
  • There's no seasonality information, though rain genuinely changes what's possible.
  • The premium segment is capped by permit limits, so your growth has to come from the rest of the country — where your data is weakest.
Can it fit?

It fits in spirit, but not yet in the details.

The values line up already. The gap is accuracy, not intent — and accuracy is fixable. Encoding things like Umuganda is also exactly what a foreign competitor will never bother to do.

L

Legal

Rules you must follow — some handled, some not yet looked at.

What we think You are now in reasonable shape on personal data, and in poor shape on content and commerce. Your legal position also changes category the moment you take money.

How it fits

  • Data protection under Law N° 058/2021 is genuinely implemented — people can see, download and delete their data.
  • Collecting enquiries rather than payments keeps you low-risk today.
  • The admin system already gives you a way to correct or remove a listing when a business asks.

How it doesn't fit

  • Your listing photos are pulled from other people's sites. Some need crediting; some are almost certainly not licensed at all.
  • Taking payment for someone else's tour makes you a travel middleman, with licensing and refund obligations attached.
  • The privacy policy points at a mailbox nobody reads.
  • Earning money brings business registration and tax duties you haven't looked at.
Can it fit?

It fits today. It stops fitting the day you monetise.

Two things to do before that day: audit where your photos come from, and check what taking payments requires. Both are cheap now and expensive once you're trading.

So — can Tembera fit or not?

Yes. The environment is unusually favourable, and none of the poor fits are permanent.

Four of the six forces already fit or nearly fit. The two that don't each have one identified change that flips them: make it work offline, and sort out photo licensing before taking money. Neither is a rewrite. Neither is expensive today.

Economic fits — one model only

Technological not yet — offline flips it

But it fits as one specific thing, not as anything you like. Tembera fits as a curated directory working alongside the national tourism effort, serving high-value visitors, the diaspora, and — once it speaks Kinyarwanda — Rwandans travelling their own country. It does not fit as a mass-market consumer app funded by advertising. The economics and the language barrier would defeat that version, and no amount of good engineering would rescue it.

The deciding factor is the same one the SWOT landed on from a completely different direction: verified place data. It is what makes the institutional partnership possible, what an AI assistant cannot replicate, what a foreign competitor won't bother to encode, and what the conference-visitor segment actually needs. Two frameworks arriving at the same answer by separate routes is the strongest signal either one can give you.
